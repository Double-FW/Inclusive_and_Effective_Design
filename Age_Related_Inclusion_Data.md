# Age-Related Disability Prevalence by Age Group (UK, US, EU and CAN Oriented Baselines)

## Purpose - Indicative UK-oriented prevalence estimates for a range of age-related disability groups across broad age demographics.

The figures are designed to support:

- accessibility strategy
- inclusive design planning
- demographic modelling
- service accessibility forecasting
- policy and organisational planning
- AI and accessibility research

These figures are not intended to function as clinical prevalence statistics. Different organisations measure disability differently and many conditions overlap. Some figures are derived from direct prevalence datasets while others are interpolated from broader age-band studies and national disability surveys.

---

# Disability Prevalence Estimates by Age Group

| Age Group | Hearing Impairment / Hearing Loss | Sight Loss / Vision Impairment | Blindness / Severe Sight Impairment | Upper-Body / Dexterity Impairment | Cognitive / Memory Impairment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0–20 | ~0.3–1% | ~0.2% | <0.05% | ~1–2% | ~1–2% |
| 21–40 | ~1–3% | ~0.3–1% | ~0.01% | ~3–5% | ~2–4% |
| 41–60 | ~15–25% | ~1–3% | ~0.02–0.05% | ~5–7% | ~4–5% |
| 61–80 | ~45–65% | ~10–20% | ~0.1–0.3% | ~12–14% | ~7–8% |
| 80+ | ~75–80%+ | ~25–50% | ~0.5–1%+ | ~18–22% | ~10–12% |

---

# Interpretation Notes

## Hearing Impairment

Hearing impairment is one of the most strongly age-correlated disabilities.

Key trends include:

- prevalence rises significantly from approximately age 50 onward
- prevalence accelerates sharply after age 70
- mild hearing loss is substantially more common than severe hearing loss
- many individuals do not formally identify as disabled despite functional impact

By the age of 70+, hearing loss becomes one of the most common sensory impairments within the population.

---

## Vision Impairment and Blindness

Sight loss also increases substantially with age.

Key patterns include:

- relatively low prevalence in younger demographics
- strong increase from approximately age 60 onward
- severe sight impairment and blindness remain comparatively rare
- mild and moderate vision loss are considerably more common than formal blindness

Many older adults experience reduced contrast sensitivity, reduced peripheral vision, cataracts, glaucoma, or age-related macular degeneration without necessarily being classified as blind.

---

## Upper-Body / Dexterity Impairment

Upper-body motor impairment includes:

- reduced dexterity
- arthritis-related limitations
- tremor
- reduced grip strength
- fine motor impairment
- shoulder, arm, wrist, or hand limitations

These impairments increase steadily with age and can significantly affect interaction with:

- touch interfaces
- keyboards
- remote controls
- mobile devices
- gaming controllers
- authentication systems
- physical service environments

---

## Cognitive and Memory Impairment

This category includes:

- age-related cognitive decline
- memory impairment
- processing speed reduction
- executive function decline
- dementia-related impairment

The prevalence of mild cognitive limitation increases gradually with age, while dementia prevalence accelerates strongly in later life.

Many individuals experience functional cognitive barriers without having a formal diagnosis.

---

# Important Research Considerations

## Overlap Between Conditions

These categories are not independent.

Older populations frequently experience:

- combined hearing and vision impairment
- combined motor and cognitive impairment
- multiple concurrent accessibility barriers

This overlap is extremely important when designing:

- digital products
- customer journeys
- healthcare systems
- public services
- media systems
- gaming experiences
- AI interfaces

---

## Disability Identification vs Functional Limitation

Formal disability registration figures significantly underrepresent actual prevalence.

Many people:

- do not seek diagnosis
- do not register impairments
- do not identify as disabled
- experience situational or progressive impairment

Functional accessibility requirements are therefore often substantially larger than official disability statistics suggest.

---

## Accessibility Implications

The most significant age-related accessibility growth areas are:

1. hearing accessibility
2. cognitive simplicity
3. visual clarity
4. motor accessibility
5. multimodal interaction support

Older populations increasingly require:

- captions
- simplified navigation
- larger interaction targets
- stronger contrast
- clearer typography
- lower cognitive load
- multimodal interaction options
- reduced reliance on precision motor control

---
